For both of us · Prepared September 2026

He wants to start a YouTube channel

Everything I think we'd have to agree on before saying yes or no, in one place.

He's asked about starting a YouTube channel — Roblox, playing with his friends. I've looked into what that actually involves rather than answering off the top of my head, and a few things turned out to matter more than I expected. Rather than decide anything on my own, here's the whole picture so we can work through it together.

Nothing has been posted and he hasn't been told yes. I've set up the account itself so we're not held up by logistics if we agree — it's on my Google account rather than his, which is the normal arrangement for a child's channel, and you'll have the login. That's groundwork, not a decision. If the answer is no, it gets deleted and that's the end of it.

The part I didn't know, and think you should

"Made for Kids" is a legal designation, and it lands on us

Every upload gets marked either "made for kids" or not. Content aimed at children is supposed to be marked as such — and Roblox gameplay aimed at other kids almost certainly is.

Worth reading twice

After YouTube's $170m settlement with the FTC over children's privacy, individual channels — not YouTube — are treated as responsible for their own compliance with the US children's privacy law (COPPA). Whichever of us owns the account carries that. It's the reason this isn't a setting to guess at.

Marking it correctly switches a lot off: comments, live chat, the notification bell, saving to playlists, channel memberships and personalised ads. Two consequences worth separating:

  • Good for us: no comments means no strangers talking to him under his videos. That removes what I'd otherwise consider the single biggest risk.
  • Bad for him: it removes most of the tools channels use to grow, and the advertising is worth far less. He should know this going in rather than find out.

Realistically, this earns nothing

To earn ad money at all a channel currently needs 1,000 subscribers plus either 4,000 watch hours in a year or 10 million short-video views in 90 days. From February 2027 those last two figures double for anyone applying new. A channel starting now would very likely be held to the higher bar.

So: this should be treated as a hobby that teaches him something, not as income. If it ever did make money, we'd want to have agreed beforehand where it goes — and some US states have laws about earnings by child performers that we'd need to look at properly at that point.

What I'd propose as the ground rules

These are a starting position, not a fait accompli. Change anything.

Identity — nothing that locates him
  • A channel nickname, not his full name. First name only at most.
  • No face on camera. None of the video formats he'd be making need it — his Roblox character is the character on screen.
  • Never named or shown: his school, our town, the street, the lake, the house, anything with an address or a number plate in shot.
  • Voice is fine — that's most of what makes these videos work — but voice plus a location is the combination to avoid.
Friends
  • Their parents are asked before that friend appears, and told plainly it's a public YouTube channel.
  • If a parent says no, that friend isn't in it — and he doesn't get to negotiate with them about it.
  • If the channel ever gets real attention, we go back and re-ask. Consent to something small isn't consent to something large.
Supervision
  • A parent watches every video end to end before it's public. This catches the accidental — a friend using his real name, a stray bit of screen, something said in the background.
  • Both of us can see the account and take anything down without needing the other's help.
  • We agree a time budget up front, and it doesn't come out of school nights.
  • We set a date to review honestly — my suggestion is three months — including the option of stopping with no hard feelings.

What he'd actually be making

For context on what this looks like in practice, the format that does well in this corner of YouTube is simple and, I think, genuinely harmless: he and a friend play a game with one silly rule added — one of them blindfolded while the other gives directions, or nobody allowed to talk. The entertainment is the two of them arguing and laughing.

No camera, no scripts, nothing he has to perform. He records with the tablet's built-in screen recorder; I've set up something that finds the good bits so he isn't spending hours editing. Realistically it's one afternoon a week.

Questions I don't have answers to

How many hours a week, and does it pause for school?

One thing I'd ask either way

Whatever we decide, I'd like us to give him the answer together and give him the actual reason. If it's no, he should hear why rather than just hearing no. If it's yes, he should understand that the rules aren't us being difficult.
